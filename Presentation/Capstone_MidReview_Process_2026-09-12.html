03 · PROBLEM STATEMENT
03 / 19

A static, one-size-fits-all buffer policy in a business that isn't static

CPG companies carry excess stock on the wrong SKUs while stocking out on the SKUs that matter — a structural planning failure, not a forecasting one.

Today
  • One safety-stock formula for thousands of SKUs
  • A blanket ~99% service target, set once, rarely revisited
  • Lead-time variability averaged away
  • No feedback loop from outcomes back to parameters
Supply Brain's Objective
  • 01Segment SKUs
  • 02Set differentiated service policies
  • 03Decide how much buffer to hold & where to position it across the network
  • 04Keep re-tuning that decision as outcomes come in

Working Capital

Right-size safety stock to each segment's real demand risk — investing more where risk is high, less where it isn't.

Service Level

Protect high-margin, critical SKUs via differentiated targets & multi-echelon positioning.

Write-offs

Reduce waste on high-variety, demand-uncertain SKUs via segment-aware planning.

Strategic IP

A deployable, self-tuning MEIO framework Accenture can take to CPG clients.

04 · WHAT WE'RE BUILDING
04 / 19

Supply Brain: a MEIO system for buffer positioning across the DC network

MEIO = Multi-Echelon Inventory Optimization — decide, for every SKU at every stocking node, how much safety stock to hold and where in the network to hold it, instead of one blanket policy everywhere.

500 SKUs
Across 5 categories, replenished through a 4-echelon network — Plant → Plant DC → Regional DC → Customer DC.
23 nodes
8 Regional DCs + 15 Customer DCs actually carry demand/inventory in this dataset, fed by Plant DCs.
Closed-loop
Segment SKUs, set the policy, position the buffer, then keep re-learning from outcomes — not a policy frozen after one setup.

08 · PHASE 2 · SEGMENTATION
08 / 19

Why we cluster before we optimize

•

500 SKUs cannot each get a bespoke policy by hand, and a single blanket policy ignores real differences in margin, demand volatility, forecastability and supply risk.

•

Clustering compresses that complexity into a small number of archetypes management can actually review and sign off on — a segment is a decision unit, not just a modeling convenience.

•

Each cluster gets its own recommended service-level target and, downstream, feeds MEIO's per-segment safety-stock policy — segmentation is the bridge between raw SKU data and a policy stakeholders can reason about.

•

Built to be statistically defensible, not just visually plausible: every feature that survives into the clustering is tested for significance, and the number of clusters is chosen by reproducibility, not just by eye.

11 · FROM SEGMENTS TO A STOCKING PLAN
11 / 19

Model building — MEIO turns policy into numbers

•

Segmentation tells us what target to aim for per segment (e.g. 98% for Flagship Core, 88% for the long tail).

•

MEIO turns that target into an actual number for every SKU at every one of the 23 stocking nodes: how much safety stock, what reorder point, what order-up-to level.

•

This is where the network structure (Plant DC → Regional DC → Customer DC) and each SKU's real demand and lead-time variability enter the calculation — not just its segment label.

14 · PHASE 3 · NEXT
14 / 19

Making the model responsive, not a once-a-year snapshot

Today — Yearly Model
  • One run per year, against the full 2026 forecast
  • Output: a single SS / ROP / order-up-to figure per SKU-DC for the whole horizon
  • Doesn't react to a demand spike, a late shipment, or a forecast revision mid-year — this year's recommendation sits frozen until the next annual run regardless of what changes
Next — Weekly Model
  • Re-run on a rolling weekly cadence against the latest forecast + last week's actuals
  • Output: a stocking decision for the upcoming week — how much to order, updated reorder point — not just an annual number
  • Directly implements the charter's closed-loop, self-recalibrating requirement, and is what the bandit layer needs for a fresh reward signal every round

15 · PHASE 3 · MULTI-ARMED BANDIT TUNER
15 / 19

Why a learning loop, not a one-shot optimization

•

MEIO has hyperparameters — the safety factor, review cadence, positioning template, service-level tier per segment — that a one-shot optimization fixes once from historical data and never revisits.

•

A multi-armed bandit (MAB) instead runs repeated simulation rounds across different hyperparameter settings ("arms"), observing a reward each round, and balances two things every round:

  • Exploitation — favor the setting that has worked best so far.
  • Exploration — keep testing other settings, in case conditions have shifted.
•

Recommended algorithm: Thompson Sampling — it keeps a belief distribution per arm rather than a single average, which doubles as an easy-to-explain "confidence" for stakeholders, and naturally keeps re-checking settings instead of locking in early.

What the agent is planned to do

Same principle throughout: the agent explains and proposes; a person confirms before anything real changes — a tuning setting, a simulation scope, a service-level number going into an SLA conversation.

Built on top of the bandit engine and segmentation output from Phase 3 — this layer has no independent decision logic of its own; it's a conversational front-end over decisions those models already make or can simulate.
