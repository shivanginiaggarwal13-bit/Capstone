1 · Load & prepare the order book¶

We keep only 2025 transactions — the single year of real actuals in this dataset (2026 rows are forecast). Each order line is joined to its SKU's category, price tier and unit cost.

The elasticity model needs genuine price variation. Two sources of it exist in the order book: base_price_usd drifts within each SKU across the year (list-price movement), and discount_pct is non-zero on ~5.7% of 2025 lines (max depth 35%) — promotional movement. Both signals are modest in this dataset, which is a real constraint on how precisely elasticity can be pinned down (see §7). The regression separates the two via ln(base_price) and ln(1 − discount_pct).

2 · Methodology — the elasticity model¶

Specification. A constant-elasticity (log–log) demand curve estimated with two-way fixed effects:

$$\ln(\text{qty}) \;=\; \beta_0 \;+\; \beta_1\,\ln(\text{base\_price}) \;+\; \beta_2\,\ln(1-\text{discount}) \;+\; \alpha_{\text{sku}} \;+\; \gamma_{\text{period}} \;+\; \varepsilon$$

  • $\beta_1$ = elasticity_base — % change in units for a 1% change in shelf (list) price.
  • $\beta_2$ = elasticity_promo — response to promotional depth specifically. Because the term is $\ln(1-\text{discount})$, a deeper discount lowers that regressor, so a negative $\beta_2$ means "discounts lift volume".
  • $\alpha_{\text{sku}}$, $\gamma_{\text{period}}$ — SKU and ISO-week fixed effects, absorbed by demeaning each variable within sku_id and within period before OLS. This strips out (a) the fact that structurally cheap SKUs also sell in higher unit counts, and (b) whole-market weeks where everything moved together (seasonality, holidays). What remains is within-SKU, within-week price response.

Why log–log: $\beta_1$ is the elasticity directly, constant across the price range, and the coefficient is unit-free — comparable across categories.

Estimator. We reproduce the demeaning routine from segmentation_v2.py verbatim so the point estimates match the committed pipeline, then fit OLS on the demeaned data with HC1 heteroskedasticity-robust standard errors to attach confidence intervals and p-values. (SEs are mildly optimistic — they don't debit degrees of freedom for the absorbed fixed effects — but with n in the tens of thousands the interval widths are tiny either way.)

def fixed_effects_demean(df, value_cols, group_cols):
    # Sequential within-group demeaning - copied from segmentation_v2.py section 2.
    out = df.copy()
    for col in value_cols:
        for g in group_cols:
            out[col] = out[col] - out.groupby(g)[col].transform("mean") + out[col].mean()
    return out

def estimate_elasticity(g, label, min_obs=200):
    g = g[(g.base_price_usd > 0) & (g.order_qty > 0) & (g.discount_pct < 1)].copy()
    n = len(g)
    if n < min_obs:
        return None
    g["ln_qty"]           = np.log(g.order_qty)
    g["ln_base_price"]    = np.log(g.base_price_usd)
    g["ln_one_minus_disc"] = np.log(1 - g.discount_pct + 1e-6)
    dm = fixed_effects_demean(
        g, ["ln_qty", "ln_base_price", "ln_one_minus_disc"], ["sku_id", "period"]
    )
    X = sm.add_constant(dm[["ln_base_price", "ln_one_minus_disc"]])
    m = sm.OLS(dm["ln_qty"], X).fit(cov_type="HC1")
    ci = m.conf_int().loc["ln_base_price"]
    return {
        "label": label, "n_obs": n,
        "elasticity_base":  m.params["ln_base_price"],
        "se_base":          m.bse["ln_base_price"],
        "p_base":           m.pvalues["ln_base_price"],
        "ci_base_lo": ci[0], "ci_base_hi": ci[1],
        "elasticity_promo": m.params["ln_one_minus_disc"],
        "se_promo":         m.bse["ln_one_minus_disc"],
        "p_promo":          m.pvalues["ln_one_minus_disc"],
        "r2": m.rsquared,
    }

The segmentation case the charter is built to catch — Dairy & Bakery. At category level it reads as moderately elastic (−1.25). Split by tier, that average hides two opposite behaviours: Premium Dairy & Bakery is genuinely elastic (≈ −1.7) while Value-tier Dairy & Bakery is not statistically distinguishable from zero elasticity (β₁ ≈ −0.29, p ≈ 0.21, CI crosses zero) — the flattest demand curve in the portfolio. 5 · Benchmark sanity-check¶

The elasticity is derived from real discount variation, not assumed — but we still cross-check the regression output against published FMCG elasticity ranges (Obsidian concept note → literature) so a mis-specified model would stand out.

    ("Staples (fruit/veg)",              -0.80, -0.26),

6 · The price–inventory exchange rate¶

The question. For each category, is it cheaper to absorb demand uncertainty by holding safety stock, or by holding price flexibility (running a discount to pull demand in when needed)?

Construction. We take the safety-stock dollars the MEIO optimiser already recommends (meio_summary_by_category.csv, so nothing is fabricated) as the fixed quantity of protection, and price both ways of buying it:

| step | formula | |---|---| | annual inventory cost | optimized_SS_usd × (holding_cost%_annual + cost_of_capital%) | | safety-stock in units | optimized_SS_usd / avg_unit_cost (category mean) | | extra demand needed, % of base | SS_units / Q0, where Q0 = 2025 category unit volume | | discount depth required | |ΔQ% ÷ elasticity_base| | | annual price-buffer cost | discount_depth × avg_price × Q0 | | exchange rate | inventory cost ÷ price-buffer cost |

exchange rate < 1 → inventory is the cheaper buffer; > 1 → price flexibility is cheaper. The discount is modelled as applying to the whole baseline volume (the standard category-promo assumption — you can't discount only the marginal buyer without targeting).

cost_p  = pd.read_csv(f"{DATA}/cost_parameters.csv")

          .agg(Q0_units=("order_qty", "sum"), revenue_usd=("revenue_usd", "sum"))
          .reset_index())

                     .rename("avg_unit_cost_usd").reset_index(), on="category")

def routing(x):
    if x >= 3.0:  return "Price-dominant (≥75% price)"
    if x >= 1.5:  return "Price-leaning blend (50/50 → 25/75)"
    if x >= 0.67: return "Balanced blend (~50/50)"
    return "Inventory-dominant (≥75% inventory)"

rows = []
for _, r in base.iterrows():
    e = cat.loc[r.category, "elasticity_base"]
    carry = r.holding_cost_pct_annual + r.cost_of_capital_pct
    inv_cost = r.optimized_ss_usd * carry
    ss_units = r.optimized_ss_usd / r.avg_unit_cost_usd
    dq = ss_units / r.Q0_units
    dp = abs(dq / e)
    price_cost = dp * r.avg_price_usd * r.Q0_units
    xr = inv_cost / price_cost
    rows.append(dict(
        category=r.category, elasticity_base=round(e, 3),
        optimized_ss_usd=round(r.optimized_ss_usd), current_ss_usd=round(r.current_ss_usd),
        carrying_rate=round(carry, 3), inv_annual_cost_usd=round(inv_cost),
        ss_units=round(ss_units), Q0_units=int(r.Q0_units),
        discount_needed_pct=round(dp*100, 2), price_buffer_cost_usd=round(price_cost),
        exchange_rate=round(xr, 2), routing=routing(xr),
    ))

Result. At the safety-stock magnitudes MEIO recommends, inventory is the cheaper buffer for four of five categories. Frozen Foods is the sole category near parity (1.23×, price marginally cheaper). Portfolio-wide the exchange rate is ≈ 0.33 — every $1 spent discounting to pull demand in as a buffer offsets only about 33¢ of inventory carrying cost.

This is not because the categories are inelastic — Frozen and Snacks are highly elastic. It's the base-rate effect: the safety stock is only ~1–1.6% of annual volume, but the discount needed to shift that 1–1.6% is paid on all the volume, whereas carrying cost is paid only on the buffer units themselves. Frozen Foods escapes it through a low average price ($5.95) and a small safety-stock base relative to throughput; Beverages is the worst case ($36 average price × ~2M units × a 1.1% discount dwarfs its carrying cost).

7 · Findings, routing recommendations & caveats¶

Findings¶

  1. All five categories are price-elastic (β₁ from −1.06 to −3.60); Frozen Foods & Snacks are highly elastic, Beverages least.
  2. Tier matters. Dairy & Bakery splits from elastic (Premium ≈ −1.7) to near-inelastic (Value ≈ −0.3). Category-level elasticity is not a safe routing input on its own.
  3. The exchange rate favours inventory for Beverages, Grocery, Dairy & Bakery and Snacks; Frozen Foods is the one break-even case. Portfolio exchange rate ≈ 0.33×.

Routing recommendation (buffer-mix starting point per segment)¶

Category Exchange rate Recommended buffer mix
Frozen Foods 1.23× Trial a 25–50% price-buffer blend — closest to parity, highly elastic, low ticket price
Snacks & Confectionery 0.65× Inventory-led; price as a secondary lever on Value tier
Grocery / Dairy & Bakery / Beverages 0.21–0.44× Inventory-led. Reserve price moves for clearance / write-off avoidance, not routine buffering. For Dairy & Bakery, treat Premium and Value tiers as different SKUs.

Caveats¶

  • Model R² is low (0.008–0.062). Price explains only a sliver of order-to-order quantity variation on this synthetic data. The sign, ordering and rough magnitude across categories are the reliable outputs; the point estimates carry more uncertainty than the tight CIs imply.
  • Thin price variation. Only ~5.7% of 2025 lines carry a discount (max 35%), so elasticity_base leans heavily on modest within-SKU list-price drift. Value-tier Dairy & Bakery in particular comes back statistically insignificant.
  • Uniform-discount assumption. The price-buffer cost assumes the discount hits all baseline volume. A targeted / dynamic-pricing capability (the charter's own agentic layer) would discount only the at-risk slice and move every exchange rate toward price.
  • One year of actuals. A second year would let the fixed effects separate seasonal price patterns from true elasticity more cleanly.
  • One-shot estimate. The Obsidian research track proposes upgrading this into a self-recalibrating exchange rate via a multi-armed bandit over discrete buffer-mix arms — a stronger version of the same deliverable, not required here.
  • Exchange rate is anchored to MEIO's optimised safety stock. Against the pre-optimisation policy the inventory side is ~60% larger (see current_ss_usd), which shifts the rates but not the ranking.
